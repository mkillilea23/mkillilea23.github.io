Reflecting

Finding My Place in Instructional Design

Deciding to pursue instructional design felt less like a career pivot and more like finally putting a name to the type of work I have been drawn to for years. When I first began exploring graduate programs, I found myself torn between people-centered fields like education and psychology, and more traditionally “practical” options such as business administration. I wanted a career that felt meaningful but also versatile and grounded in real-world skills. In discovering instructional design, I found that it blends all of these priorities. It offered a way to leverage my creativity and passion for helping others with my analytical and problem-solving skills, and it quickly became clear that this field aligned with both how I think and how I want to work.

My background in speech language pathology and academic advising has shaped how I approach learning design. I regularly utilize my assessment, planning, and intervention skills I developed through my time in speech pathology to create learning experiences grounded in evidence-based learning theory. As an advisor, I have learned how to meet students where they’re at and produce individualized plans that help support their goals, a strength that is valuable in needs analysis and developing learning objectives.

Across these experiences, the part of my work that has consistently resonated with me is helping people make sense of complex information and helping them master the skills necessary to address whatever challenges they are facing. Seeing their confidence grow or the moment when things “click” is incredibly rewarding, and that is reflected in my design work. I aim to facilitate true learning that results in real change, rather than just checking the boxes.

One of the takeaways I’ve gathered from this program so far has been realizing how often performance deficiencies are a design problem rather than a people problem. This insight directly informed projects like my training on writing quality advising notes. The goal was not simply to “do better,” but to define what quality means and clarify expectations. Coursework emphasizing research-backed practice and analysis-based design has pushed me to continue to step back and ask better questions rather than jumping straight to solutions.

Looking ahead, my goal is to transition into a role as an instructional designer, ideally within higher education or mission-driven environments, though I am also open to corporate opportunities. I am especially interested in work that supports individuals during periods of transition, where access to clear and accurate information matters most. Ultimately, I want to design learning experiences that are practical, equitable, and genuinely helpful; work that makes challenges easier to navigate and helps people gain confidence in their abilities.
